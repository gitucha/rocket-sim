How the physics works

01Forces on the rocket

The rocket flies straight up, so everything is one-dimensional. Up is positive. At each instant three forces act:

F = T − D − m·g(h)   ⇒   a = F / m

T is engine thrust, D is aerodynamic drag (always opposing motion), and m·g(h) is weight. While the rocket sits on the pad, the ground pushes back, so it only lifts off once T > m·g. That ratio is the thrust-to-weight ratio:

TWR = T / (m₀·g₀)

A TWR below 1 means the rocket burns fuel while sitting on the pad. Real launch vehicles usually lift off with a TWR of about 1.2–1.5. Sounding rockets often go higher.

02Thrust, Isp and changing mass

The sim holds thrust constant while a stage has fuel. Specific impulse (Isp, in seconds) measures how efficiently the engine turns propellant into thrust. It sets the mass flow rate:

ṁ = T / (Isp · g₀)

where g₀ = 9.80665 m/s². The product Isp·g₀ is the effective exhaust velocity vₑ. As propellant burns, the mass drops while thrust stays the same, so acceleration rises through the burn. That's why the velocity curve bends upward until burnout.

burn time = m_fuel / ṁ

Typical Isp values: solid motors about 230–270 s, kerosene/LOX 280–340 s, hydrogen/LOX about 450 s in vacuum.

03Drag and the atmosphere

D = ½ · ρ(h) · v² · Cd · A     A = π·(d/2)²

Air density falls off roughly exponentially with altitude. The sim uses an isothermal model with a scale height of 8.5 km:

ρ(h) = 1.225 · e^(−h / 8500)  kg/m³

By 30 km the density is about 3% of its sea-level value, and by 60 km it's about 0.09%. So drag matters a lot early in the flight and hardly at all once you're high up.

The term q = ½ρv² is the dynamic pressure. It rises as the rocket speeds up and then falls as the air thins. Its peak, Max Q, is the moment of highest aerodynamic load on the structure, which is why real vehicles often throttle down around it. The sim only records Max Q on the way up, the way it's quoted for real launches.

04Gravity that weakens with altitude

g(h) = g₀ · ( R / (R + h) )²     R = 6 371 km

At 400 km, gravity is still about 89% of its surface value. Astronauts on the ISS float because they're in free fall, not because there's no gravity. The sim also checks escape velocity once all stages are spent:

v_esc = √( 2·g₀·R² / (R + h) )

If the rocket is moving upward faster than this with no thrust left, it never comes back, and the sim ends the flight as ESCAPED EARTH.

05Staging and the rocket equation

The ideal velocity change a stage can give, with no gravity and no drag, comes from Tsiolkovsky's rocket equation:

Δv = Isp · g₀ · ln( m₀ / m_f )

Here m₀ is the mass at ignition and m_f is the mass at burnout. The important point: m₀ includes everything above the stage, meaning the upper stages and the payload. The sim works this out for each stage and adds them up to give the total ideal Δv shown under the sliders.

The logarithm is why staging works. Once a stage burns out, its empty tanks and engine are dead weight. Dropping them means the next stage doesn't have to accelerate that mass any further. When a stage burns out, the sim drops its dry mass, coasts for 1.5 s, and ignites the next stage.

Worked example: the default two-stage rocket

Stage 1 Stage 2
Thrust / Isp 70 kN / 230 s 12 kN / 250 s
Dry + fuel 1000 + 2400 kg 250 + 450 kg
m₀ (incl. everything above) 4200 kg 800 kg
m_f 1800 kg 350 kg
TWR at ignition 1.70 1.53
Burn time 77 s 92 s
Ideal Δv 1.91 km/s 2.03 km/s

That's 3.94 km/s of ideal Δv in total, plus a 100 kg payload. In the sim this rocket reaches an apogee of about 392 km.

Why bother staging? Build a single-stage rocket with the same total mass and the same total propellant (1250 kg dry, 2850 kg fuel, 70 kN). Its ideal Δv drops to 2.56 km/s and it only reaches about 150 km. Same propellant, less than half the altitude, because it drags its empty tanks all the way up.

06Where the Δv goes: gravity and drag losses

The default rocket has 3.94 km/s of ideal Δv, but it's only moving at about 2.06 km/s when stage 2 burns out. The missing 1.87 km/s is lost to two things:

  • Gravity loss: for every second the engine fires straight up, gravity takes back about g·Δt. With roughly 170 s of burning, that's about 1.6 km/s. This is why higher TWR (a shorter, harder burn) wastes less, and why real rockets pitch over into a gravity turn.
  • Drag loss: set Cd to near zero and the burnout speed rises to about 2.29 km/s and the apogee to about 477 km. So drag costs this rocket roughly 230 m/s.

07Numerical integration

The equations of motion are stepped forward with a fixed time step dt = 0.01 s using semi-implicit (symplectic) Euler:

v ← v + a·dt   →   h ← h + v·dt

Updating velocity first and then using the new velocity for position keeps the energy behaviour much better than plain Euler, at no extra cost. A convergence check on the default rocket:

dt Apogee
0.1 s 392.70 km
0.01 s 391.90 km
0.001 s 391.84 km

The result changes by about 0.02% going from 0.01 s to 0.001 s, so the default step is accurate enough. Time warp just runs more steps per animation frame. It never makes the step bigger.

08Assumptions and limits

  • Vertical flight only. There's no pitch-over or gravity turn, so the rocket can't reach orbit. Orbit needs about 7.8 km/s of horizontal velocity.
  • Constant thrust and Isp. Real engines gain thrust as outside pressure drops, and Isp differs between sea level and vacuum.
  • Constant Cd. Real drag coefficients change a lot with Mach number and peak near Mach 1.
  • Simple atmosphere. An exponential model, not the full US Standard Atmosphere 1976.
  • Same diameter for every stage when working out drag.
  • No Earth rotation, no wind, and no parachute: descent speed is set only by drag (about 80 m/s at impact for the default rocket).

Each of these is a possible upgrade. See the roadmap in the README.
